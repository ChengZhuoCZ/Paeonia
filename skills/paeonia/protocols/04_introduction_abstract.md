# Protocol 04 — Reader-First Introduction and Abstract

## Authority

This protocol is the ordinary entry point for front matter. It **simplifies and governs** the older Introduction modules.

For an ordinary Introduction, do not instantiate every narrative ledger, story graph, ambition ledger, scientific-context dossier, paragraph-role table, amplification pass, and editorial-impact report. Those are optional repair or research modules. Use them only when the trigger in §9 is present.

The default path for **Introduction reconstruction** is:

`closed theorem truth → search and verify related literature (04u) → fair-credit / exact-frontier map → IntroductionCoreBrief → default evidence-grounded program test (04v) → conditional PDE extension as applicable → one selected arc → direct prose → reader, credit, and program cold reads → precise revision`.

The new research pass is required when external access is available. It distinguishes **discovering omitted scholarship** from verifying existing bibliography items, and it is completed before prose. The default program test is an internal decision, not permission to invent a grand claim. `LOCAL_POLISH` retains scoped evidence checking and prose preservation. These additions do **not** instantiate every optional narrative ledger.

A good Introduction is not the one that completes the most internal artifacts. It is the one from which the intended reader can recover the problem, frontier, theorem, obstruction, central idea, and significance with minimal interpretive work.

## 1. Scope and mathematical gate

For full-manuscript reconstruction, finalize the Introduction after the proof core and reader-facing theorem statement are semantically stable. For an Introduction-only reconstruction or local revision, the supplied theorem and proof may be used as trusted evidence; do not force a new proof-body draft.

Respect the selected writing mode:

- `LOCAL_POLISH`: preserve the author’s successful prose, sequence, and voice; repair only demonstrated reader problems.
- `RECONSTRUCT`: rebuild the authorized Introduction from the mathematics and verified literature. The source Introduction supplies claims, terminology, citation candidates, and authorial priorities, but it does not dictate the new paragraph sequence.

No mode may invent mathematics, novelty, history, or citations.

## 2. Research first, then build the IntroductionCoreBrief

In `RECONSTRUCT`, before building the final brief or drafting the Introduction, perform [Protocol 04u](04u_literature_discovery_and_credit.md): search relevant original mathematical articles using more than the inherited bibliography, assign fair credit for the origin, direct predecessors, canonical benchmarks, partial frontiers, method ancestry and contemporaneous/parallel work, and verify pivotal claims against the source version. Check the recent literature at the research date. Record one compact `IntroductionLiteratureDossier`, including search scope and unresolved evidence. Do not claim a novelty or priority check is complete if only citation keys or abstracts were checked. If outside search is unavailable, document the limitation and narrow claims. This pass is scoped to affected facts for `LOCAL_POLISH` and may not rewrite protected prose.

Before drafting prose, compress the paper into the following internal brief. Each item should normally fit in one or two sentences.

1. **Exact question.** What mathematical question does the paper answer, in the smallest natural class and normalization?
2. **Benchmark.** Which theorem, principle, model, or conjecture makes this question natural?
3. **Pre-paper frontier.** Which relevant result or incomparable frontier nodes locate the problem, and what exact gap remained?
4. **Main theorem.** What changes after this paper, at the strongest verified scope?
5. **Nonroutine obstruction.** Why does the benchmark theory or natural route not already prove the result?
6. **Conceptual hinge.** What new relation, representation, reduction, or estimate overcomes that obstruction, and what control does it unlock?
7. **Meaning and limits.** What does the theorem change in the mathematical picture, and what does it not claim?
8. **Verified scholarly ancestry and fair credit.** Which verified authors and works established origin, conjecture, benchmark, closest predecessors, partial or incomparable frontiers, competing or parallel results, and method ancestry? Which claims need direct attribution, and which citations can be grouped because they perform the same mathematical job? Do not impose a numerical citation cap.

The brief is the controlling object. If a proposed paragraph cannot be traced to one of these items or to a necessary definition for the theorem, it needs a specific justification.

Do not convert the eight items into eight paragraphs. They are facts to compose, not prose slots.

### 2A. Conditional PDE extension

When the theorem is governed by a PDE, free-boundary problem, geometric PDE, stability mechanism, or singular PDE limit, apply [Protocol 04t](04t_pde_introduction_branch.md). Extend the same core brief with only the operative theorem coordinates: equation and setting, regime, solution class, multi-axis result delta, result architecture, assumption roles, a capped causal obstruction stack, mechanism-to-control bridge, and typed scope boundaries. Do not instantiate a separate planning stack and do not force every field into prose.

For PDE papers, the pre-paper frontier may be a partial order rather than one globally strongest theorem. Record incomparable frontier nodes when they improve different axes. The narrative still has one primary arc; the result architecture may nevertheless be a dichotomy, stratification, hierarchy, bridge, or solution-class separation.

## 3. Test a genuine grand mathematical program, then choose one narrative arc

Use [Protocol 04v](04v_evidence_grounded_grand_narrative.md) by default for reconstruction: test the source-backed path from canonical principle and parent program through the pre-paper frontier and exact obstruction to the theorem and its larger mathematical meaning. **Prefer an earned programmatic opening, not ceremonial brevity.** If the parent program has no precise role, use a strong direct opening. This internal test is lightweight; the extended MathematicalStoryLedger, NarrativeAmbitionLedger, grand outline and scientific context ledgers remain optional.

Select the arc that makes the theorem easiest to understand. Typical choices are:

- **benchmark → restriction → lost mechanism → theorem**;
- **conjecture → strongest frontier → missing case → theorem**;
- **exact problem → obstruction → theorem → conceptual hinge**;
- **phenomenon → mathematical formulation → theorem → explanation**;
- **limiting/asymptotic theory → exact finite or global gap → theorem**;
- **scientific model → operative regime → mathematical frontier → theorem**;
- **method-first**, only when the new representation or framework is itself the main contribution.

For a weak, flat, or high-stakes Introduction, sketch two genuinely different six-line arcs and choose one before drafting. Do not write two full Introductions and merge them. Select the arc with the clearest problem pressure, earliest intelligible theorem, and strongest non-generic mathematical relation.

No arc is mandatory. In particular, do not manufacture a parent program, failed method, scientific motivation, theorem ladder, or re-expansion merely because an older template contains one.

## 4. The opening and theorem latency

The opening should establish a specific mathematical object, benchmark, or tension. Avoid field-level ceremony that could begin hundreds of unrelated papers.

Use the **swap test**:

> If the paper’s nouns were replaced by generic placeholders, would the paragraph still fit many unrelated papers?

If yes, the paragraph is too generic. Replace prestige, age, breadth, or “considerable attention” with a concrete mathematical relation.

The theorem should appear as soon as it can be stated accurately and readably. There is no page-one rule. A theorem on page 2 is natural when the preceding paragraphs are needed to establish the benchmark, finite or global restriction, exact frontier, and minimal notation. Before the theorem, every paragraph must pass the **indispensability test**:

> What fact needed to understand the theorem would become unavailable if this paragraph were removed?

History, applications, and adjacent problems do not earn pre-theorem space merely by being interesting.

## 5. Compose paragraphs by information change

A paragraph may integrate history, literature, obstruction, and interpretation when they jointly perform one coherent mathematical move. Several functions may coexist in one paragraph when they support that same information change. Do not force one database-style role per paragraph.

Each paragraph should nevertheless create a visible information change, for example:

- a classical principle becomes a constrained problem;
- a known theorem leaves a precise gap;
- an asymptotic picture is separated from exact rigidity;
- a natural method is shown to lose the decisive structure;
- the main theorem closes that gap;
- a new representation explains why the closure is possible.

The end of a paragraph should create the reader’s next mathematical question. Use that question, not an administrative transition, to begin the next paragraph.

Paragraph length and sentence rhythm follow the burden of the argument. Do not equalize them for visual regularity.

## 6. Literature as mathematical positioning

Organize citations by the distinct achievements and mathematical roles the theorem story needs. Search widely **before** deciding what belongs in the prose; provide enough credit for origins, close prior theorems, useful ancestors and relevant parallel results. There is no hard cap on references. A useful literature sentence has the form

`verified mathematical status → citation support → consequence for the present problem`.

Group works that support the same proposition. Separate them only when their mathematical roles differ. A literature paragraph is justified only if it establishes one of:

- the benchmark;
- the exact pre-paper frontier;
- the obstruction or insufficiency of an existing route;
- a genuine conceptual or technical precursor;
- a neighboring result that changes how the current theorem is understood.

Avoid a paragraph whose main logic is author chronology. Avoid repeated statements that earlier papers “do not cover” the present result; **state positively what each pivotal work establishes** and identify the remaining gap once. Place sufficiently specific attribution in the Introduction, not only in the `.bib`. Before using `first`, `settles`, `new`, `extends`, or `long-standing`, verify the exact scope and the relevant primary papers. Different frontier axes need not have a single strongest predecessor. Include contemporaneous or independent parallel developments when mathematically material; do not erase them to simplify the plot. Apply Protocol 07 to each actual citation and adjacent claim.

## 7. State and interpret the theorem

Use at most the functions the paper needs:

1. **orientation** — one concise sentence explaining what changes;
2. **formal statement** — the exact theorem with minimal notation;
3. **interpretation** — a non-obvious consequence, strength comparison, or conceptual meaning.

Do not repeat the theorem in prose after the display. Do not bury the strongest theorem beneath auxiliary refinements. A theorem ladder is used only when the later result is genuinely stronger, more global, sharper, or structurally different and the bridge is explained. For PDE papers, choose among `DIRECT_PRECISE`, `MODEL_THEN_PRECISE`, `INFORMAL_THEN_PRECISE`, and `RESULT_FAMILY` according to prerequisite burden; do not flatten a threshold dichotomy, stratification, or estimate-to-application hierarchy into a generic single-result announcement.

Strong language is evidence-sensitive. State `sharp`, `global`, `complete`, `first`, `resolves`, or `settles` when verified at the exact scope. Do not weaken a proved central result into vague “progress,” and do not enlarge its scope for impact.

## 8. Explain the proof at the right resolution

The proof overview should reveal the argument’s conceptual hinge and the few dependencies needed to understand why it works. It is not a compressed table of contents and not an inventory of every estimate.

For each indispensable stage, state only:

- the input available at that point;
- the operation or idea that changes the problem;
- the output that makes the next stage possible.

Open the central bottleneck; compress routine modules. Include a formula only when it lets the reader see a decisive sign, exponent, scaling, equality case, or compatibility between two objects. Do not foreground denominators, cutoff bookkeeping, case labels, or proof-local notation unless they are themselves the conceptual difficulty.

A useful proof overview often has two resolutions:

- one paragraph explaining the conceptual bridge;
- one paragraph explaining the remaining global, nonlinear, endpoint, or rigidity work.

This is optional, not a fixed template.

For PDE papers, expose only the proof interfaces that carry the novelty—for example local theory → continuation criterion → a priori bound; linear mechanism → nonlinear bootstrap → asymptotic closure; concentration compactness → critical element → rigidity; blow-up analysis → profile classification → stratification; or compactness topology → defect/multiplicity control → limit identification. Name standard machinery compactly and open the interface at which the paper changes the theory.

## 9. Default research/program tests and optional deep modules

**Protocol 04u is the default external scholarly research pass** for reconstructed Introductions; **Protocol 04v is the default lightweight programmatic-narrative test** after research. Neither is an optional historical expansion. These two passes create one compact evidence dossier and annotations in the ordinary core brief, not the old large planning stack. Live search limitations must be disclosed. In `LOCAL_POLISH`, apply them only to changed in-scope citation, contribution or historical claims.

Use the older modules only when they solve a demonstrated problem. Protocol 04t is different: it is a conditional domain branch inside the same core brief, not an extended narrative module.

- **Protocol 04t — PDE Introduction branch:** trigger when PDE-specific theorem coordinates, a partially ordered frontier, a branching result, or a nonlinear closure interface materially affects readability. Populate only the applicable fields; never expand a clean short Introduction merely because it concerns a PDE.

The optional narrative modules are:

- **Protocol 04n/04o — larger mathematical story:** trigger only when the theorem’s significance genuinely depends on a parent program or a nontrivial transition such as continuum→finite, local→global, or asymptotic→exact. Usually one clear arc is enough; no mandatory “program return” paragraph.
- **Protocol 04k/04l — scientific context:** trigger only when the model, regime, or physical mechanism changes problem legibility or theorem interpretation.
- **Protocol 04p/04q — deep reformulation and proof explanation:** trigger when a central representation or long proof cannot be understood from the compact proof overview.
- **Protocol 04h/04j — editorial positioning:** trigger for a verified conjecture resolution, classification, first global result, sharp theorem, or serious under-positioning. Use it to sharpen exact claims, not to add ceremonial scale.
- **Protocol 04a–04d compatibility artifacts:** use only for unusually complex multi-theorem or literature-heavy Introductions. Their ledgers are planning aids, not mandatory prose geometry.
- **Protocol 04i — amplification:** not part of the default route. Apply only after identifying a specific, evidence-backed under-positioning defect. Never broaden context merely because the draft sounds modest.

When optional modules disagree with this protocol, this protocol controls ordinary composition. A module may add depth but may not override the core brief, theorem indispensability, swap test, or reader contract.

## 10. Reader-contract cold read

Freeze the complete draft and read it without consulting the proof body. The intended specialist should be able to answer:

1. What is the exact problem?
2. What benchmark or prior theorem makes it natural?
3. What precisely was unknown?
4. What exactly is proved here?
5. Why is it not a routine consequence of known theory?
6. What is the central idea that makes the theorem possible?
7. What changes in the mathematical picture, and what is the result’s scope?

Add three scholarship/program questions for a reconstructed Introduction:

8. Who did the pivotal earlier work, what exactly did they accomplish, and is that contribution clearly credited?
9. What real larger mathematical program, if any, supplies the opening and what precise scale/category transition connects it to this theorem?
10. Are the strongest prior results, independent/parallel contributions, and the precise novelty boundary represented without unsupported priority or historical omissions?

Then run five negative tests:

- **swap test:** no substantial paragraph should remain valid after merely swapping subject nouns;
- **detachability test:** removing a paragraph must remove a needed fact, relation, or inference;
- **latency test:** no pre-theorem paragraph exists solely to create grandeur or chronology;
- **resolution test:** the proof overview explains the bottleneck but omits bookkeeping;
- **voice test:** the Introduction reads as a mathematical argument, not as ledger fields converted into sentences.

For a PDE paper, also run the applicable conditional checks in Protocol 04t and `checklists/pde_introduction_reader_contract.md`: theorem coordinates, multi-axis result delta, assumption roles, closure interface, topology, and typed boundaries. Repair the earliest missing inference. Do not respond to a weak Introduction by automatically adding more history, more literature, more named stages, more equations, or more adjectives.

## 11. Introduction output

Produce:

- the final Introduction;
- an internal `IntroductionCoreBrief`;
- an internal `IntroductionLiteratureDossier` for research-backed Introduction reconstruction (or an explicit search-access limitation);
- a short `ReaderContractAudit` listing any unresolved reader inference or factuality limit.

`IntroductionNarrativeLedger`, `MathematicalStoryLedger`, `NarrativeAmbitionLedger`, `ScientificContextLedger`, and `IntroductionParagraphPlan` are optional and should not all be generated for an ordinary task.

## 12. Abstract

Use Protocol 04s for abstract calibration. The Abstract should compress the causal relation among problem, result, and central idea without importing the Introduction’s historical arc or full proof itinerary.

No citations unless field norms strongly require them. No unsupported novelty claim. No “we investigate/study” filler when a direct mathematical statement is possible.

## 13. Consistency

Every theorem, mechanism, scope claim, equality statement, novelty sentence, historical assertion, and citation in the Introduction or Abstract must map to closed mathematics or verified scholarship. The front matter may clarify and position the result; it may not silently strengthen it.
